The port nobody taught them to fix

New nurses on an inpatient oncology unit were passing their skills check-off and then getting stuck at the bedside when an implanted port didn't work as expected. This is a discovery project: how I confirmed the problem was real, worked out what kind of problem it was, and proposed a fix with clear measures of success. It's a proposal and hasn't been implemented.

Context

An implanted port is a small device under the skin that gives direct access to a large vein. On oncology units it's how patients receive chemotherapy, blood products and lab draws. Accessing a working port is routine. Troubleshooting one that won't give blood return or resists flushing is a different skill, and getting it wrong can delay treatment or harm the patient.

Discovery

It started with one observation. A new graduate nurse who had passed the central line check-off got stuck changing a port needle, and the unit educator had to step in. One case could be a fluke, so I checked it against four other sources:

  • Senior nurses said newer nurses came to them with port problems 2–3 times a week, most often for no blood return or resistance when flushing.
  • The check-off checklist validated routine access, flushing and removal only. It had no troubleshooting scenarios at all.
  • New nurses in the fellowship program said they didn't feel confident handling a port that didn't behave as expected.
  • The unit's escalation policy set out when a nurse must call the provider, and that only nurse managers may give the de-clotting medication.

Together these showed the need was recurring, affected every new cohort, and was invisible to the current orientation process.

Framing the problem

What it looks like
TodayNew nurses can access a port but call a senior nurse when it malfunctions, 2–3 times a week across the unit. Their confidence is low.
Where we want to beThe nurse checks port function before each use, never forces a flush, rules out mechanical causes (a clamp, kinked tubing, a needle not fully seated, patient position), and escalates to the provider when blood return isn't restored.
The gapTroubleshooting and escalation are neither taught nor tested in orientation.

I classified this as primarily a skill gap, with a smaller knowledge component. The new nurses knew the procedure and could tell something was wrong. What they lacked was practice carrying out a troubleshooting sequence under real conditions. That distinction drove the solution toward hands-on repetition rather than more reading.

Root causes

  • The check-off could be passed without troubleshooting. The process designed to catch the gap was hiding it.
  • Port malfunctions are common for the unit but rare for any one nurse, so new nurses rarely get to practice.
  • Senior nurses fix the problem instead of coaching through it. That protects the patient in the moment but means the new nurse never builds the skill.
  • Staffing and competing priorities leave little room for teaching in the moment.

Proposed solution

Training alone wouldn't fix a problem partly caused by how the unit works, so the proposal has two parts.

A 75-minute simulation for each new cohort

In the skills lab, using port training mannequins: a 15-minute briefing and review of a one-page troubleshooting algorithm, 40 minutes of three scenarios drawn from what senior nurses actually reported, and 20 minutes of structured debriefing and evaluation. The last scenario has learners practice calling the provider using SBAR, a standard structure for handoff conversations.

Changes to how the unit works

  • Add troubleshooting scenarios to the check-off.
  • Post the troubleshooting and escalation algorithm at the bedside.
  • Encourage senior nurses to coach new nurses through a problem rather than take over.

How success would be measured

  • Primary: each learner completes a new scenario scored against a checklist. Critical items are pass/fail: don't use a port without blood return, never force a flush, and escalate when troubleshooting doesn't work.
  • Confidence: learners rate their confidence before and after.
  • Behavior on the unit: senior nurses tally port troubleshooting calls from new nurses for four weeks before and after. Cohorts are small, so this would be read with caution, but it ties the training back to the problem that started it.

Grounded in the Infusion Nurses Society (INS) standards of practice and Oncology Nursing Society (ONS) access device guidelines.
