Closing the expedited loophole

Expedited prior authorization requests are meant for cases where waiting could seriously harm a member's health. A well-meant workaround in our review process taught providers to mark routine requests as expedited. After we fixed the incentive behind it, staff noticed about 90% fewer unnecessary expedited requests.

  • ~90%fewer unnecessary expedited requests, as noticed by staff
  • 3groups changed: reviewers, providers, intake

The problem

We saw a surge in expedited requests. Expedited cases jump the queue, so the volume kept reviewers from getting to authorizations due that day, and timeliness started to slip. The team was stretched and distressed.

The root cause: a workaround that rewarded the wrong thing

The official workflow said that when a request didn't qualify for expedited review, the reviewer should run the expedited script and send the provider a letter denying expedited review. To avoid writing that letter while staying CMS compliant, reviewers had started deciding these cases the same day instead.

Providers noticed. Marking a request "expedited" got it decided immediately, even when it was nothing like life-threatening: colonoscopies, cataract surgery, physical therapy. The workaround rewarded exactly the behavior that was overloading reviewers.

The volume wasn't really a staffing problem. It was an incentive problem, and our own workaround had created it.

What we shipped

We changed things for everyone involved, all at once, so the loophole closed from every direction.

Reviewers

Job aids and training rolled out right away for the corrected workflow: run the expedited script; if expedited review is denied, change the authorization's priority in both places it's recorded, send the provider the letter denying expedited review, and call the member to let them know. Reviewers stopped deciding downgraded requests the same day, so the shortcut no longer paid off.

Providers

Supervisors called the providers who used the loophole most.

Intake

When a provider calls in a request, contact center agents now repeat the criteria for expedited review, and providers must attest that the request is life-threatening.

Outcome

Staff noticed about 90% fewer unnecessary expedited requests. This is an observed estimate from the team, not a formally measured figure. Requests from endoscopy centers, physical therapy providers requesting therapy for chronic pain, and cataract surgery dropped significantly, and reviewers felt the difference in their caseloads.

What I took from it

When a queue suddenly grows, look at what the system is rewarding before adding people to work it. Here the fix wasn't more capacity. It was making the rule and the incentive point the same way.
