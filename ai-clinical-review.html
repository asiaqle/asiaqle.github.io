Concept

AI-assisted clinical review

A product concept for utilization management review: what a model could prepare before a nurse opens a case, where the nurse still has to decide, how it could go wrong, and how to measure it. It builds on my out-of-network decision path and my prior auth intake recommendation. This is a concept. It isn't built or piloted.

The review today

I review Medicare Advantage and D-SNP prior authorization requests against coverage guidelines every day. A typical review looks like this:

  1. Triage. Accept the authorization in my work queue, export the queue to a spreadsheet, and work the cases due today, the overdue ones and Part B requests first.
  2. Administrative checks. Member eligibility, the provider's participating status, the member's authorization history, and the request itself: procedure codes, units and dates of service. Out-of-network requests add the checks from my decision path, like network status and driving distance to an in-network provider.
  3. Clinical completeness. Is the clinical documentation complete enough to review?
  4. Criteria match. Search the MCG module by keyword or procedure code to find the guideline that applies, then work through its criteria checklist to reach a coverage determination.
  5. Documentation. I write my clinical review note the whole way through.
  6. Close-out. Post the note, set the decision, generate the approval letter, and send it to the coordinators to fax and close the authorization.

Most of the time goes to looking things up and reading. The judgment is in steps 3 and 4.

The model prepares the case. The nurse makes every decision.

Who can say no

In utilization management, a nurse reviewer can approve a request that meets criteria but cannot deny one. Requests that don't meet criteria go to our internal medical directors, and specialty requests go to a contracted review company. When clinicals are still missing after both outreach attempts on the due date, the case goes to a medical director too. The medical director makes the final determination on any denial.

The model never denies. Its strongest output is "criteria appear met, here's the evidence," and a nurse confirms that. Anything else gets routed to a medical director or specialty reviewer with a summary attached. A model mistake can slow a case down. It can't deny care.

How it fails, and how the workflow catches it

The first two failures are ones I've seen in nurse reviews today. The rest are risks a model adds.

FailureHow the workflow catches it
Missing something buried in the clinicalsEvery criterion links to its evidence. A criterion with no evidence shows as "not found," never as "met," so the gap stays visible.
Using the wrong guideline in the hierarchyThe model works the hierarchy in order: NCD (national coverage), then LCD (local coverage), then MCG, then company policy and the member's evidence of coverage. It shows where it stopped and why, and the nurse confirms the guideline before seeing the checklist.
Made-up evidenceA citation has to open to the real text in the record. If it doesn't, the claim isn't shown.
Rubber-stampingThe nurse marks the checklist independently, and the tool compares those answers with the model's. Agreement rates are audited; a reviewer who agrees 100% of the time gets a second look.
Wrong member or wrong documentName, date of birth and member ID on the clinicals are matched to the authorization, and any mismatch is flagged before review.
Outdated guidelineThe guideline version and effective date are shown and checked against the date of service.

The trust test

As a reviewer, this is what would make me rely on a draft, and missing any of it would make me stop:

  1. Everything in the draft matches the authorization request when I double-check it.
  2. The information is accurate and pertinent to the case.
  3. Another reviewer reading it understands clearly and concisely why the decision was made.
  4. The guideline hierarchy and CMS regulations are followed.

These are the acceptance criteria. Each one maps to a measure below.

How I'd measure it

The same four-tier pattern as my intake recommendation: speed measures paired with guardrails like overturn rate on appeal, so faster reviews can't come at the cost of worse ones. Accuracy is checked by sampling and auditing cases, not by trusting the model's own claims. There's no baseline yet, so no targets are set.

How I'd roll it out

  1. Shadow mode. The model drafts in the background while nurses review as usual. Compare its drafts with their decisions.
  2. A small group of reviewers sees the drafts. Track the guardrails and the trust test closely.
  3. Expand only if the guardrails hold. If overturns, reversals or mismatches rise, stop and fix before going further.
